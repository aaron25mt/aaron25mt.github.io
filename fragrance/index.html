01 · The foundation

How a fragrance unfolds

A perfume is a timed composition. The first impression is not the whole scent; volatile materials lift first, then the heart and base take over as it dries.

The note pyramid
TopFirst minutes · citrus, herbs, light fruit
HeartThe character · florals, spice, aromatics
BaseThe memory · woods, amber, musk, vanilla

Important: a listed “note” is often an impression or accord—not a literal ingredient. The pyramid is a storytelling model, not a stopwatch.

N

Note

A recognizable smell idea: bergamot, cedar, coffee, sea air. It can come from natural materials, aroma molecules, or both.

A

Accord

Several materials blended to create one impression—like leather, amber, tobacco, or ocean spray.

D

Drydown

What remains after the bright opening fades. This is the phase you live with longest, so test beyond the first five minutes.

F

Flanker

A related release sharing a name and idea. EDT, EDP, Parfum, “Intense,” and “Elixir” may be different compositions—not just stronger versions.

Concentration

Strength is not a scoreboard

These labels usually signal how much fragrance concentrate sits in the carrier. They are conventions—not globally fixed rules—and they do not guarantee projection or longevity.

Ef

Eau Fraîche / Splash

Very light, refreshing, and made for a brief scented lift.

~1–3%
EdC

Eau de Cologne

Brisk, airy and often citrus-led. Made for refreshing reapplication.

~3–8%
EdT

Eau de Toilette

Often brighter and more diffusive than richer versions of the same line.

~5–15%
EdP

Eau de Parfum

Typically fuller and denser, with more emphasis on the heart and base.

~10–20%
P

Parfum

Usually concentrated and smooth; it may sit closer to skin than an EDT.

Varies by brand
Ex

Extrait

The richest conventional format, often emphasizing texture and depth.

~15–40%

Rule of thumb: compare versions on skin. Formula, materials, climate, spray count, and skin chemistry matter more than the letters on the bottle.

Performance language

Three different questions

“Strong” is vague. Ask whether a fragrance pushes outward, leaves a trail, or simply remains detectable for a long time.

Radius

Projection

How far the scent radiates from your body—especially in the opening hours. Think personal bubble.

Trail

Sillage

From the French for “wake”: the scented trace you leave as you move through a space.

SprayHours later
Duration

Longevity

How long you can detect it on skin. A quiet skin scent can last all day without projecting far.

Why reports disagree: dose, weather, skin, fabric, formula changes, and nose fatigue all shift perceived performance. Ask for a range, not a universal hour count.

02 · The scent map

Families are directions, not boxes

One fragrance can be aromatic, woody, spicy, and amber at once. Use families as a shared vocabulary for the dominant impression.

to deepA practical map—not a universal standard

The Michael Edwards wheel is a major reference with 14 families. This guide adds common modern shopping language—such as “blue,” gourmand, tobacco, and oud—to make the map useful in real conversations. Here, amber means a warm resinous accord—not ambergris.

03 · Note atlas

Train your nose with contrasts

Do not memorize giant note lists. Start with opposites: bright vs. dark, dry vs. sweet, airy vs. dense. Then notice the materials that create the effect.

Bright

Citrus

Bergamot is tea-like and elegant; lemon is sharper; grapefruit can feel bitter or sulfurous.

Fresh

Herbs

Lavender, sage, mint, rosemary, and geranium add lift, cleanliness, and aromatic structure.

Juicy

Fruit

Apple, pineapple, plum, pear, and berries bring sweetness without necessarily becoming dessert-like.

Dry

Woods

Cedar feels crisp and pencil-dry; sandalwood creamy; vetiver grassy, rooty, smoky, or earthy.

Warm

Resins

Labdanum, benzoin, and myrrh create the golden, balsamic warmth often described as amber.

Textural

Spice

Cardamom feels cool and aromatic; cinnamon warm and sweet; pepper bright, dry, and prickly.

Soft

Iris

Powder, lipstick, clean fabric, carrot, or suede—iris can feel polished and quietly luxurious.

Skin

Musk

A broad family of clean, soft, warm, laundry-like, or sensual effects that anchor and blur a scent.

05 · How to wear

Application without the cloud

There is no magic number. Start lower, account for temperature and setting, then learn how your skin and the formula behave.

Neck Chest Wrist

Pick two or three zones; do not treat every dot as mandatory.

1

Spray clean, moisturized skin

Unscented moisturizer can slow evaporation. Clothing often holds scent longer, but test delicate or light fabric for staining.

2

Do not rub wrists together

Rubbing is unnecessary and can smear the opening. Let the fragrance evaporate naturally.

3

Scale to the room

Office, airplane, clinic, and restaurant: stay restrained. Outdoors and cold weather can usually tolerate more.

4

Layer for a reason

Test one spray of each scent on paper first. Pair around a shared note or simple contrast, and keep the stronger fragrance at a lower dose.

5

Respect sensitivity

Skip fragrance in scent-free spaces and around people who report migraines, asthma triggers, or sensitivity.

6

Store it cool, dark, and steady

Keep bottles away from direct sun, high heat, and rapid temperature swings. Spray away from eyes, broken skin, and open flame.

Where the map is still open

A gap is an invitation to sample—not an obligation to own. Choose only if the new lane suits how you actually dress, live, and smell.

True high-heat fresh

Lean citrus, aquatic, or green—less tonka and sweetness than your Chanel.

The one-minute recap

Smell smarter, buy slower

The best collection is not the one with every family. It is the one whose bottles earn distinct roles in your actual life.

Test the drydown

The opening sells the bottle; the base is what you wear for hours.

Sample on skin

Paper reveals structure. Skin reveals texture, diffusion, and comfort.

Match dose to setting

Conversation distance is enough for most indoor situations.

Shop by missing mood

Ask “what experience does this add?” before “do I like the opening?”

Let labels overlap

A scent can be blue, aromatic, woody, fruity, and amber at the same time.

Wear beyond the aisle

“Masculine” and “feminine” are marketing directions, not rules about who may enjoy a scent.

End note

Fragrance is a language learned nose-first.
